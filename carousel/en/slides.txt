## Slide 1

What is one vote worth?

Spain's general election of 29 November 2026

Óscar García López · November 2026

Abstract. I simulate Spain's election 100,000 times from the polls to 10 November 2026. For the average voter, one more vote changes a seat with probability 1 in 76,000, and decides whether either 2023 investiture bloc reaches 176 seats with probability 1 in 10.8 million. Every list follows the same rules, and nothing here advises anyone.

## Slide 2

The question

Spain elects 350 deputies in 52 constituencies by D'Hondt, with a 3% threshold in each. One more vote can move a seat from one list to another, or move the seat that decides the absolute majority: 176.

For the majority I use the coalitions of the two 2023 investiture votes, so no party is placed by anyone's judgement:

Bloc | Yes votes

F | Feijóo, 27 September 2023: 172 PP 137, Vox 33, UPN 1, CC 1

S | Sánchez, 16 November 2023: 179 PSOE 121, Sumar 31, ERC 7, Junts 7, EH Bildu 6, PNV 5, BNG 1, CC 1

Frente Amplio and Podemos, both in Sumar's group in 2023, count in S; lists with no seat in 2023 count in neither; Coalición Canaria voted yes twice, so it is in both. I also report S without Junts.

## Slide 3

The model

Polls: each firm's latest poll with fieldwork ending 27 October – 10 November 2026; 16 firms, none excluded. I take the median of each list.

National error: each share moves on the logit scale with σ = 0.22, the size of the same median's errors 19 days out in 2015–2023 (43 cases), centred on zero.

Constituencies: proportional swing from 2023, with regional and provincial deviations (σ = 0.11 and 0.07) estimated from past swings; turnout varies too. SALF and the split of Sumar's vote start from the 2024 European vote.

Seats: D'Hondt with the 3% threshold; for each list, Δ is the extra votes it needs for one more seat.

One poll in the window was released by a party (Vox); without it, P(F ≥ 176) is 94.8% instead of 94.2% and P(S ≥ 176) 3.7% instead of 4.2%.

## Slide 4

The chance of deciding

One vote decides a seat when a list is one vote short of it. That chance is the density of Δ at zero:

p ≈ k / (n · h) (1)

where k counts the runs, out of n = 100,000, with 0 < Δ ≤ h, and h is 2% of the constituency's votes per seat. With h from 1% to 5%, the national answers move by 0.2% (seat) and 3.2% (majority).

For the majority, only the runs where that seat moves a bloc across 176 count.

The average voter weights each constituency and list by its expected votes: it is the chance for a vote picked at random.

## Slide 5

Does it work? Backtests

I ran the same code seven days before the elections of November 2019 and July 2023, calibrated only on earlier elections. The rule for widening the errors was committed before the first run: widen if fewer than 75% of the actual seat counts fall in their 80% intervals, or fewer than 90% in their 95% ones. Result: 22 of 26 and 24 of 26, so no widening (Figure 2).

Table 1. Forecast P(≥ 176) and where the actual bloc total fell among the simulated ones. S in 2019: PSOE, Unidas Podemos, PNV, Más País, BNG.

Election, bloc | P(≥ 176) | Actual seats | Percentile

2023, F | 62% | 172 | 30%

Run 19 days out, as for 2026: 20 of 26 and 23 of 26, short of the 95% target, so the 2026 intervals may be a little narrow.

Small constituencies weigh more: 35,000 electors per seat in Teruel, 150,000 in Madrid. Ceuta, a one-seat race, is the most decisive: 1 in 10,000 for its seat, 1 in 740,000 for the majority. For the majority, Soria is the least, at 1 in 41.4 million.

## Slide 9

Who holds the power

Seats are not power. The Shapley–Shubik index is the share of the orders of forming a coalition in which a list's seats carry it to 176: arithmetic leverage, not who will govern.

## Slide 10

Does voting pay?

In the calculus of voting (Riker and Ordeshook, 1968), voting pays when

R = p B - C + D > 0 (2)

with p the chance of being decisive, B what the outcome is worth to you, C the cost (30 minutes at 10 €/h: 5 €) and D what voting is worth in itself.

With p = 1 in 10.8 million, a selfish voter breaks even only if the majority is worth 53.8 million € to them (Downs, 1957).

The players

Voters: the outcome (B) and voting itself (D).

Parties: votes, office and policy (Strøm, 1990); their leverage is Table 3.

Pollsters and media: they shape what each player expects of the others; no poll may be published in the five days before the vote (LOREG, art. 69.7).

Outside constraints, such as EU fiscal rules, narrow what any majority can do.

## Slide 11

Counting the others

If B counts everyone (Edlin, Gelman and Kaplan, 2007),

B = N b α (3)

with N = 49.9 million residents, b the gain per person and α the weight you give a stranger. Here p N = 4.6: with b = 100 €, voting pays once α passes 1.1%, about 1 in 93.

## Slide 12

Others' votes, limits, takeaways

Others' votes. Persuasion barely moves votes in general elections (Kalla and Broockman, 2018); getting people out does, in US experiments (Gerber, Green and Larimer, 2008). At an assumed 0.1 to 1.0 extra voters per hour, an hour beats 10 €/h only if the majority is worth 107.7 million € to 1.1 billion € to you, or on the social reckoning of (3).

Limits. Polls 19 days out. Proportional swing; SALF and Adelante Andalucía rest on the 2024 European vote or a flat share. Local lists can surprise. Blocs fixed by the 2023 votes.

Takeaways. A seat is a far likelier prize than the majority. Between constituencies, the odds of deciding the majority differ by a factor of up to 56. Selfishly, voting rarely pays; counting others, it can.
